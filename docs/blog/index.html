A Platonic — and Umwelt — reading of color bias

How a presentation-level feature such as text color enters a vision encoder's representation, read through the current debate on representational convergence.

The word does not change; only its color does. A vision encoder still places it at a new location in concept space.

1 · The question

In Seeing Red, Thinking Bad we render a sentence as an image and recolor one word (e.g., turning excellent green) without altering a single character. The string is identical; only the visual styling differs. Vision language models still shift their sentiment judgment, and a CLIP probe shows that sweeping a rendered word's hue moves its image embedding along interpretable semantic axes (valence, safety, emotion). The encoder treats color as an informative feature, although color carries no propositional meaning here.

This is not specific to one model. Cross-modal inconsistency is documented: vision language models answer the same content differently when it is presented as text tokens versus as text rendered inside an image (Cross-Modal Consistency in MLLMs, 2024; Same Content, Different Answers, 2025). The question we ask is narrower. If independently trained encoders are thought to converge on a shared, abstract account of the world, what does it mean that a presentation-level feature enters that account? We read our finding through the current debate on representational convergence.

2 · The Platonic hope and a failure mode

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola, 2024) proposes that large models trained on different data, objectives, and modalities are converging toward a shared statistical model of reality. In the cave metaphor, each modality is a different shadow of the same object. On this view a word like terrible should occupy a fixed semantic location regardless of its color.

Read against that hope, our result is a failure of invariance. A feature that should be lexically irrelevant moves the representation. Color carries no propositional content here. It carries only learned associations, and the encoder is demonstrably sensitive to it (Color in Visual-Language Models: CLIP deficiencies, 2025). We state the scope carefully. This is a boundary case for the strong, presentation-invariant reading of the hypothesis, not a direct test or refutation of it. The paper does not measure cross-model convergence. It shows that for text-as-image, presentation-level variables enter the representation even when the string is fixed.

3 · Convergence is partial and local

The strong, global form of the hypothesis is the part recent work questions, and that work indicates where a surface feature can enter.

Gröger, Wen & Brbić (2026), in an Aristotelian revision, show that much apparent convergence under global similarity metrics such as CKA is a scale-induced artifact: the score inflates with model width and depth. After null-calibration, what survives is not a shared global geometry but agreement on local neighborhood structure, that is, which items are nearest neighbors. Koepke, Zverev, Ginosar & Efros (2026) scale the original mutual-nearest-neighbor test to millions of samples. Alignment is fragile. It collapses at scale and leaves only coarse semantic overlap, with models inhabiting modality-specific Umwelten rather than one shared reality. Hadgi et al. (2025) add that where cross-modal convergence does appear, it lives in low-dimensional subspaces that can entangle conceptually distinct axes.

The shared space, where it exists, is partial, local, and low-dimensional, rather than a single global geometry. That is the kind of space in which a surface feature can re-rank a word's neighbors.

4 · What we measured

Our probe is consistent with this account. We report it as a measurement within CLIP's embedding space, read directionally rather than as a universal magnitude, since global geometric quantities can be scale-confounded (Gröger et al.).

We find three things. First, color carries valence on its own. A plain color swatch with no word projects more positive and more safe when green than when red, which indicates that the encoder treats color as a semantic signal. Second, coloring a word is not the addition of that color vector. A word's hue-driven movement overlaps the pure-color direction by only about 8%, so color largely reshapes how that particular word is represented. This is a color-by-word interaction. Third, the hue trajectory is closed but not planar. Sweeping hue from 0 to 360° returns to its start (cosine ≈ 0.99) while spanning roughly 4 to 6 effective dimensions. The trajectory does not reduce to a flat ring, which is consistent with a coarsely shared rather than a canonical low-dimensional representation. 5 · An Umwelt reading

A second frame makes the effect expected rather than anomalous. The Umwelt Representation Hypothesis (Bosch, Sommers, Doerig & Kietzmann, 2026) argues that representational alignment reflects overlapping ecological constraints (environment, sensory apparatus, training statistics, objectives) rather than recovery of one global optimum. It cites cross-cultural color perception as evidence against universality. On this view the color-to-valence coupling is not a deviation from a Platonic ideal. It is a faithful imprint of CLIP's human-centric training data, in which color already carries learned affective and cultural meaning.

The two readings converge on the same conclusion. Under the Platonic lens the coupling is a failure of an invariance that was assumed to hold. Under the Umwelt lens it is a property of a representation shaped by a particular niche. Under both readings, a non-semantic property shifts a semantic judgment.

6 · Why it matters

Both interpretations carry the same practical consequence: a reliability and safety risk. Pipelines that read documents or interface screenshots can be influenced, benignly or adversarially, by styling that changes no words. Reasonable mitigations include normalizing rendered text before inference, cross-checking image-based answers against OCR-extracted text, and adding style-invariance checks to evaluation suites.

7 · Caveats

We use these hypotheses as interpretive lenses, not proofs. CLIP is a diagnostic encoder, a stand-in for a converged representation rather than the universal Platonic form. The ~8% overlap and the 4-to-6-dimensional loop are our own measurements within CLIP's embedding space, read directionally. "Green is positive" is a learned, culturally contingent association that we measure, not an innate ideal. None of the four convergence papers studies color. We cite them for the convergence-measurement caveat and for the local and niche-specific reframing of the hypothesis, not as evidence for color leakage itself. This page is an interpretive companion. The empirical claims live in the paper. (Version 0.1, to be expanded.)
