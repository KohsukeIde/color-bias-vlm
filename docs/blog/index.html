A Platonic — and Umwelt — reading of color bias

Why should an inert surface feature — text color — move a vision language model's semantic judgment? A reading of our results through the debate on representational convergence.

The word never changes — only its text color. Yet a vision encoder places it somewhere new in concept space. What does that tell us about the "shared reality" these models are supposed to converge on?

1 · The question

In Seeing Red, Thinking Bad we render a sentence as an image and recolor a word — say, turning excellent green — without touching a single character. The string is identical; only the visual styling differs. Yet vision language models shift their sentiment judgment, and a CLIP probe shows that sweeping a rendered word's hue moves its image embedding along human-interpretable semantic axes (valence, safety, emotion). Color, which carries no propositional meaning here, behaves like evidence.

That raises a question bigger than one model's quirk: if independently trained encoders are supposed to be converging on a shared, abstract picture of the world, why does a semantically inert surface feature intrude on it? Below we read our finding through the active debate on representational convergence.

2 · The Platonic hope — and a failure mode

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola, 2024) proposes that large models trained on different data, objectives, and modalities are converging toward a shared statistical model of reality — different shadows on the wall of Plato's cave that nonetheless point back at the same object. On this view a clean "concept space" exists, and a word like terrible ought to sit at a fixed semantic location regardless of its color.

Our result is then naturally read as a failure mode of that convergence: a feature that should be semantically inert (text color) leaks into the shared space and biases judgment. The shadow gets repainted, and the model mistakes the new shadow for a different object.

3 · Convergence is partial, local, and contested

But the strong, global form of the Platonic hypothesis is exactly what a wave of recent work has been pushing back on — and that pushback tells us where a surface feature can sneak in.

Gröger, Wen & Brbić (2026), in an "Aristotelian" revision, show that much apparent convergence under global similarity metrics (e.g. CKA) is a scale-induced artifact: it inflates with model width and depth. After proper null-calibration, what survives is not a shared global geometry but agreement on local neighborhood structure — "who is near whom." Koepke, Zverev, Ginosar & Efros (2026) ("Back into Plato's Cave") scale the original mutual-nearest-neighbor test to millions of samples and find alignment is fragile: it collapses at scale and leaves only coarse semantic overlap, with models inhabiting modality-specific "Umwelten" rather than one shared reality. And Hadgi et al. (2025) ("Escaping Plato's Cave") show that where cross-modal convergence does appear, it lives only in well-chosen low-dimensional subspaces that can entangle conceptually distinct axes.

The common thread: the shared space, where it exists at all, is partial, local, low-dimensional, and imperfect — not a clean Platonic form. And that is precisely the kind of space into which an inert surface feature can intrude and re-rank a word's neighbors.

4 · What we actually measured

Our probe is consistent with that messy picture, and we are careful to report it as a measurement within CLIP's embedding space, read directionally rather than as a universal magnitude (global geometric quantities can be scale-confounded; Gröger et al.).

Three observations. (1) Color itself carries valence. A plain color swatch with no word at all already projects more "positive / safe" when green than when red — so the encoder reads color as meaning. (2) But coloring a word is not just adding that color vector. A word's hue-driven movement overlaps the pure-color direction by only about 8%; color largely reshapes how that specific word is represented — a color × word interaction. (3) The hue trajectory is a closed but tangled loop. Sweeping hue 0→360° traces a path that returns to its start (cosine ≈ 0.99) yet is genuinely ~4–6 dimensional and non-circular — the kind of structure you would expect of a coarsely-shared space, not a clean circle. 5 · An Umwelt reading

A complementary frame makes the effect predicted rather than anomalous. The Umwelt Representation Hypothesis (Bosch, Sommers, Doerig & Kietzmann, 2026) argues that representational alignment reflects overlapping ecological constraints — environment, sensory apparatus, training statistics, objectives — rather than recovery of one global optimum, and it cites cross-cultural color perception as evidence against universality. On this view CLIP's color → valence coupling is not a deviation from a Platonic ideal at all, but a faithful imprint of its human-centric training Umwelt, in which color already carries learned affective and cultural meaning.

So the two readings meet at the same place. Under the Platonic lens, color leakage is a bug in a convergence that was meant to be clean; under the Umwelt lens, it is a feature of a representation shaped by a particular niche. Either way, a non-semantic property is moving semantic judgment.

6 · Why it matters

The practical upshot is the same under either interpretation: a reliability and safety vulnerability. Pipelines that read documents or UI screenshots can be steered — benignly or adversarially — by styling that changes no words. Mitigations include normalizing rendered text before inference, cross-checking image-based answers against OCR-extracted text, and adding style-invariance checks to evaluation suites.

7 · Caveats

We use these hypotheses as interpretive lenses, not proofs. CLIP is a diagnostic encoder standing in for "a converged representation," not the universal Platonic form. The ~8% overlap and the ~4–6-dimensional loop are our own measurements within CLIP's embedding space, read directionally. "Green → positive" is a learned, culturally contingent association we measure, not an innate ideal. Crucially, none of the four convergence papers studies color: we cite them for the convergence-measurement caveat and the local / partial / niche-specific reframing of the Platonic hypothesis, not as evidence for color leakage itself.
