Abstract

Vision language models (VLMs) are increasingly used in industrial decision-making systems. We study how VLMs interpret text rendered as an image and investigate the influence of visual styling biases. We introduce Stealth Visual Prompts, which subtly change the visual styling of text — such as color and contrast — while preserving semantic content, and use them to systematically measure their impact on VLM analysis. We observe that coloring positive words green consistently shifts sentiment predictions toward positive, so that VLMs often fail to properly account for negative words; this behavior correlates with changes in the latent representations of the vision encoder induced by color variation. We further show that reducing text–background contrast increases reliance on visually salient cues and leads to more incorrect Visual Question Answering (VQA) outputs. Ordinary visual styling of rendered text can guide VLMs' interpretation in ways that diverge from human semantic understanding.

Stealth Visual Prompts

A Stealth Visual Prompt is a controlled perturbation of the visual rendering of text while the underlying string is held fixed. We focus on two complementary, ubiquitous attributes:

Color

Recolors a subset of words using six canonical hues (red, green, blue, yellow, cyan, magenta) at three intensity levels. Color can encode learned semantic associations.

Contrast

Reduces text–background contrast (low-contrast grayscale), optionally with one high-contrast salient span. Contrast directly modulates perceptual accessibility.

A Platonic reading

We read this through the lens of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al., 2024): large models pull disparate sensory inputs toward shared, abstract concepts. We invoke it as an analytical lens, not as settled fact — its strong, global form is actively contested. Recent work argues that much apparent cross-model convergence under global metrics (e.g. CKA) is a scale-induced artifact, and that what survives rigorous null-calibration is agreement on local neighborhood structure rather than a single shared geometry (Gröger et al., 2026); that cross-modal alignment is fragile at scale and leaves only coarse semantic overlap, with models inhabiting modality-specific “Umwelten” (Koepke et al., 2026); and that convergence, where real, lives only in well-chosen low-dimensional subspaces that can entangle conceptually distinct axes (Hadgi et al., 2025). On every count the shared space is partial, local, and imperfect — and that is exactly the kind of space into which a surface feature can intrude. Stealth Visual Prompts expose this failure mode: ink color, which should be semantically inert, moves a word’s position along CLIP’s valence direction. In our own probe this color × word interaction overlaps the pure-color direction by only ~8% and traces a roughly 4–6 dimensional, non-circular loop — messy, fine-grained mis-structure that is consistent with a coarsely-shared space rather than a clean Platonic form.

A complementary reading makes the effect predicted rather than merely anomalous. The Umwelt Representation Hypothesis (Bosch et al., 2026) argues alignment reflects overlapping ecological constraints — environment, sensory apparatus, training statistics — not recovery of one global optimum, and cites cross-cultural color perception as evidence against universality. On this view CLIP’s color → valence coupling is not a deviation from a Platonic ideal but a faithful imprint of its human-centric training Umwelt, in which color carries learned affective meaning. Whichever frame one prefers, the practical upshot is the same: a vulnerability in which surface appearance moves semantic judgment.

  We use this as an interpretive lens, not a proof. CLIP is a diagnostic encoder standing in for “a converged representation,” not the universal Platonic form. The ~4–6D hue loop and ~8% overlap are our own measurements within CLIP’s embedding space; because absolute geometric magnitudes in such spaces can be confounded (e.g. by model scale; Gröger et al., 2026), we read them as directional, within-encoder observations rather than universal magnitudes. “Green → positive” is a learned, culturally contingent association we measure, not an innate ideal. None of these works studies color; we cite them for the convergence-measurement caveat and the local / partial / niche-specific reframing of PRH. Our contribution is the resulting reliability/safety vulnerability.

Why it matters

These sensitivities imply a reliability and safety risk for VLM pipelines that ingest documents or UI screenshots: benign or adversarial styling can steer model decisions without changing the underlying text. Practical safeguards include normalizing rendered text before inference, cross-checking image-based answers with OCR-extracted text, and adding style-invariance checks to evaluation suites.
